# Five years earlier

*The Masar story: one conversation, and when it happens.*

> Everyone in this story is synthetic. Masar is a hackathon prototype. It is not a medical device, not clinically validated, and not connected to Malaffi, Health Age or any real health record.

## Today

Noura is 34, an Emirati woman who works in Abu Dhabi and plans to have children "in a few years". She feels healthy. She believes what many people believe: that fertility holds steady until about 40, and that IVF can make up the difference after that.

The clues are already in the health system, just never in the same place. Her mother went through menopause at 42, noted once by a GP. A gynaecology clinic tested her AMH (a hormone used as context for egg reserve) twice, two years apart, and it fell clearly. Her Health Age check last year was fine. Nobody has looked at these together, because nobody is asked to until someone is trying for a baby.

So Noura's first real fertility conversation happens at 39, after a year of trying. By then some of her options are narrower than they would have been. Nobody did anything wrong. The system just never connected the dots in time.

## With Masar: the same story, five years earlier

**The signal.** At her Health Age check, Noura agrees to let Masar look at her records for reproductive-health signals, for her and her own doctor only. Overnight, Masar's rules notice the pattern: early menopause in the family, a meaningful fall in AMH, age 34, plans for "later". It doesn't give her a score. It raises a signal: *this is worth a conversation now rather than in five years.*

**The message.** Her phone shows "You have a new update in Masar". Nothing on the lock screen mentions fertility. She opens it and chooses what to share, source by source: lab results yes, clinic records yes, her smartwatch no.

**Before she sees anything,** Masar asks her three quick questions. One asks whether fertility stays about the same until 40. She answers yes.

**What she sees** is her own path, in Arabic, with no number on it:

- **What we know:** her cycles are steady. Her AMH fell from 2.4 to 1.3 over two years, more than normal variation.
- **What this can't tell her:** egg quality, or her chance of pregnancy.
- **What she can't change:** her age and her family history.
- **What she can change:** her weight and sleep, which are already in good shape. The advice is to keep them there.
- **Her options:**
  - learn more
  - an ultrasound to narrow the uncertainty
  - pre-pregnancy counselling
  - a conversation about preserving fertility, including egg freezing

She chooses the specialist conversation, and Masar helps her write down her questions.

**The doctor.** Dr Layla, her family physician, opens the morning's signals inbox. Noura's one-page summary shows the reasons, the evidence and what's missing, and it takes about ninety seconds to read. Dr Layla accepts the referral and adds the ultrasound. That decision is recorded, which is how the system learns whether its signals are right.

**The choice.** Two weeks later Noura sits down with a specialist. Maybe she freezes eggs. Maybe she and her husband decide to start sooner. Maybe she waits, knowing what she's choosing. Masar doesn't decide for her. Afterwards, four quick questions confirm she feels sure and informed. She now gets the "fertility until 40" question right, and her anxiety score is no higher than before. Her employer and her insurer can't see any of this, because no setting exists that would let them.

## Not just Noura

Across town, Arjun, a 37-year-old man from Kerala who works as a site engineer, gets a signal too: he smokes and has a BMI over 30, and he and his partner want children soon. Men are part of this story as much as women. Arjun is offered support to quit smoking, and a semen analysis to discuss with his doctor.

Maryam, a 29-year-old woman, is frightened by something she read online, and Masar shows her the opposite picture. Nothing recorded so far points to a problem, and Masar is honest about what it can't know.

## The health system

At the Department of Health, an analyst opens the population view. It runs on synthetic residents and hides any group too small to stay anonymous. It shows how many signals were found, how quickly people reached counselling, and how many referrals were completed.

It also shows where the system is falling short: in the demo data, men and South Asian expatriates are reached later than everyone else. That's something the system can act on, for example by adding those languages and adding prompts for men to Health Age checks.

The view also reports on its own accuracy: how often doctors agreed with a signal, and how often someone told "nothing to discuss" later turned out to need help.

## What the judges see

The demo is four minutes of this exact story, running on synthetic data. Every outcome in the brief appears once, in this order:

1. noticed earlier
2. change shown over time
3. explained in plain language
4. options offered
5. reviewed by a clinician
6. privacy protected
7. results measured

And one line on the final slide:

> **Without Masar, Noura's first fertility conversation happens at 39. With Masar, it happens at 34, and she's the one who decides what to do with it.**
